How the prophet works

What the prophecy is made from, and how a second model, which forecasts what the edits will change, has fared on six months of past days.

← Back to the prophecy

The prophecy

Every night just after midnight UTC, Zoroaster reads the day's edits to English Wikipedia and ranks about one and a half million recently edited pages by how likely each is to burst the next day: far more edits than usual for that page, from at least two editors (accounts editing dozens of pages a day are left out).

The ranking comes from a gradient-boosted model trained on three years of Wikipedia's edit history. It sees only each page's own recent activity and the calendar, not the news. Pages already busy are likelier to stay busy, but most of tomorrow's bursts are new, so most of the prophecy won't come true: in testing, about one in five of the top 100 did.

Forecasting the edits

A second model, still being tested and not yet part of the prophecy, forecasts what a page's edits will change: which sections they'll touch, and what kinds of change they'll make (new prose, references, tables, links and so on). It's a small language model, Qwen2.5 with 1.5 billion parameters, fine-tuned on 14,500 past page-days. For each page it reads the page as it stood the night before: its opening, its section headings, what changed that day, and the burst signals.

Its forecasts are scored against what editors actually changed, and against a simple rule: expect yesterday's changes again. On 2,796 page-days from December 2025 to June 2026, none of which it saw in training, it foretells the kinds of change better than that rule, and the sections about as well. Section precision: the share of the sections a forecast names that did change. Main section named: how often it names the section that changed most. Kinds of change: how well the kinds forecast match the kinds that happened, from 0 to 1 (the Jaccard index). The prophet names three sections a day and the rule about two, which is most of why the prophet names the main section more often.

Every forecast, next to what happened

Machine-generated forecasts, not facts. Each one is a model's guess at what Wikipedia's editors would change on a page that day, shown next to what they actually changed. A forecast makes no claim about any person or event.

Section names the model made up aren't shown, and neither are sensitive ones: health, death, crime, legal matters and personal life. New text isn't quoted for pages about living people. bold green marks the section that changed most, green what changed or happened, and red what didn't.

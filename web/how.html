The prophecy

Every night just after midnight UTC, the prophet reads two kinds of evidence, both as they stood at the end of the day before, so it can't know what the day itself brings:

  • The pages likeliest to burst with editing that day (below), biographies left out. Each page comes as it stood the night before, with every date in it worked out relative to the day foretold: "October 4 [in 4 days]".
  • The week's stories on Wikipedia's Portal:Current events: each story reported in the two days before, with its reports from the week.

A language model running on the prophet's own machine (Qwen2.5 with 7 billion parameters, in 4-bit) then takes two steps for each page or story. First it asks what will be decided within the next seven days, and when: a final, an election, or, for a story, its likeliest next development. Then it writes one prediction, "I predict that …", with a due date and its confidence.

Separate questions to the same model check every prediction, and any one of them can drop it:

  • People: a prediction may name no individual, living or dead, nor point to one by a title such as "the president of France".
  • Organizations: none may do or suffer harm, or be blamed for something, and in a war, a disaster or a crime none may be named at all. A country's government or armed forces, named by the country ("Ukraine's armed forces"), stand for the country, which may be named. A prediction dropped only for a person or an organization is rewritten in general terms ("an armed group", "a major bank"), keeping where it happens, and checked again.
  • Already known: a prediction that restates what its evidence already settled is dropped.
  • Made-up details: a story's prediction that gives a number or a name its reports don't goes back to the prophet to revise, and is checked again.

At most ten predictions a day are published, world events first, and sport only when it settles a title. Each is graded on its due day against what Wikipedia then reports. The grades are still checked by hand while the automatic judge is being tested, so the site doesn't show them yet.

The pages likeliest to burst

The prophet's first model reads the day's edits to English Wikipedia and ranks about one and a half million recently edited pages by how likely each is to burst the next day: far more edits than usual for that page, from at least two editors (accounts editing dozens of pages a day are left out).

The ranking comes from a gradient-boosted model trained on three years of Wikipedia's edit history. It sees only each page's own recent activity and the calendar, not the news. Pages already busy are likelier to stay busy, but most bursts are new, so most of the list won't come true: in testing, about one in five of the top 100 did. A listed page is a statistical forecast of editing activity, not a claim that anything has happened or will happen.

Forecasting the edits

A second model, still being tested and not yet part of the daily prophecy, forecasts what a page's edits will change: which sections they'll touch, and what kinds of change they'll make (new prose, references, tables, links and so on). It's a small language model, Qwen2.5 with 1.5 billion parameters, fine-tuned on 14,500 past page-days. For each page it reads the page as it stood the night before: its opening, its section headings, what changed that day, and the burst signals.

Its forecasts are scored against what editors actually changed, and against a simple rule: expect yesterday's changes again. On 2,796 page-days from December 2025 to June 2026, none of which it saw in training, it foretells the kinds of change better than that rule, and the sections about as well. Each score covers every day, then the pages the burst model ranked highest:

Section precision: the share of the sections a forecast names that did change. Main section named: how often it names the section that changed most. Kinds of change: how well the kinds forecast match the kinds that happened, from 0 to 1 (the Jaccard index). The prophet names three sections a day and the rule about two, which is most of why the prophet names the main section more often.

Every forecast, next to what happened

Machine-generated forecasts, not facts. Each one is a model's guess at what Wikipedia's editors would change on a page that day, shown next to what they actually changed. A forecast makes no claim about any person or event.

Section names the model made up aren't shown, and neither are sensitive ones: health, death, crime, legal matters and personal life. New text isn't quoted for pages about living people. bold green marks the section that changed most, green what changed or happened, and red what didn't.
